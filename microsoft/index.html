What happened, in order

No single cause did this. Three conditions — one of them a day old, one of them months old — met at the primary gateway at the worst hour of the week.

THE DAY BEFORE
An unrelated issue leaves the primary gateway running nearly 14% short on capacity. The shortfall is not restored before the next peak.
SINCE JULY 2026
A fix has been deploying across the fleet since July. Inside it sits a latent bug — lock contention inside an internal process — dormant for months because nothing has pushed the process hard enough to fire it.
THU NOV 12 · AFTERNOON US
A Teams network gateway fails. Its traffic shifts onto the primary gateway — which is already short on capacity — during peak US hours.
THE CASCADE
The primary gateway keeps admitting traffic it cannot serve. The overload triggers the latent lock-contention bug. Services fail across Outlook, Teams, Copilot 365, SharePoint, and OneDrive in 70+ countries.
FRI NOV 13 · 16:10 UTC
Services fully recovered — almost a full day after the failure began.

Root cause by root cause: what the Gate does

The Gate sits at ingress, in front of the thing being protected. Its law never changes: measure the residual, admit what fits, refuse the rest, record the refusal. Here is that law applied to each of Microsoft's three root causes.

1

A gateway failed, and its load landed on the primary

Root cause one — the trigger

What happened

When the Teams network gateway failed, its traffic moved to the primary gateway. The primary accepted all of it. There was no point in the path where excess load could be turned away, so a single gateway failure became a fleet-wide overload.

One failure, inherited by everything behind it.

What the Gate does

The Gate measures the primary's residual capacity in real time and admits failover traffic only up to that residual. Everything above it is refused at ingress and recorded — turned away cleanly, with a reason, instead of being admitted to die inside.

Result: graceful degradation instead of cascade. Some requests are refused; the service stays up for the rest.

2

The primary was already running ~14% short

Root cause two — the hidden shortfall

What happened

An unrelated issue from the previous day left the primary gateway nearly 14% below its nominal capacity. Planning and failover assumed the full number. The system was weaker than anyone's dashboard said it was — and the shortfall only became visible when the extra load arrived.

The margin everyone counted on was not there.

What the Gate does

The Gate never admits against nominal capacity. It admits against the measured residual — nominal minus whatever is already gone. A gateway at 86% has a residual of 86%, and that is the number the Gate enforces, whether the missing 14% was lost yesterday or a minute ago.

Result: the shortfall shows up as a smaller admission limit before the crisis, not as a collapse during it.

3

The overload woke a bug that had slept since July

Root cause three — the latent bug

What happened

Under the overload, an internal process hit lock contention — a bug carried in a fix deploying since July. It had run for months without incident because the load that triggers it had never arrived. When it finally did, there was no quick rollback: the bug was woven through months of deployment.

A dormant defect became the outage's amplifier.

What the Gate does

The Gate caps admitted load at the measured residual — below the overload the bug needs in order to fire. The latent bug stays latent, not because it was found, but because it never receives the load that triggers it. And if load ever does approach the line, the refusals are already in the ledger, timestamped, before anything breaks.

Result: the bug never gets its trigger, and the blast radius of anything downstream stays bounded.

The Gate law, stated plainly

Four steps, in order, for every arrival. Nothing is admitted on trust, on nominal figures, or on hope.

Step 1
Arrival
A request, a failover, a burst — load presents itself at ingress.
→
Step 2
Measure the residual
Real remaining capacity, right now — not the nameplate number.
→
Step 3
Admit what fits
Load inside the residual is admitted and served normally.
→
Step 4
Refuse & record the rest
Load beyond the residual is refused, and the refusal is written to the ledger.

A refusal is not a failure. A refusal is the system working — a bounded, recorded, explainable "no" at the door, instead of an unbounded collapse inside. The service degrades gracefully: fewer requests served, all of them served well, and a complete record of every one that was turned away.

That record matters. Because every admission and every refusal is written as it happens, the ledger is the post-incident report — the timeline, the shortfall, and the moment load crossed the line are already documented, not reconstructed afterward from fragments.

Stated honestly

What the Gate does not do

The Gate does not fix the latent bug.

The lock-contention defect in Microsoft's internal process would still be there, in the code, exactly as it has been since July. No admission control repairs another system's software, and this page does not claim otherwise.

What the Gate does is narrower, and it is enough: it keeps admitted load below the threshold where the bug fires, so the defect never receives its trigger — and it bounds the blast radius when anything downstream does fail, because load beyond the residual was already refused and recorded at the door instead of piling up inside. The bug remains Microsoft's to fix. The day-long, 70-country cascade was not the bug's doing alone — it was what happened when nothing stood between the bug and unlimited load.

The ledger writes the report

Microsoft's preliminary report arrived days after recovery, reconstructed from the inside of a failed system. A gated system produces its account as it goes.

Every decision, recorded at the moment it is made

  • The measured residual at the time of each admission decision — so a capacity shortfall is a number in the record, not a discovery made afterward.
  • Every refusal: what was turned away, when, and how far beyond the residual it was.
  • The sequence — arrival, measurement, admission or refusal — in order, end to end, for any single request.

What that buys in an incident

When something still goes wrong — and the honest claim is bounded, not impossible — the questions a post-incident report exists to answer are already answered: When did capacity drop? When did load cross the line? What was refused, and what got through?

No reconstruction. No archaeology through a system that was too overloaded to log its own failure. The record was written by the same mechanism that prevented the cascade.
